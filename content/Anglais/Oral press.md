Hello, a significant event took place last Thursday, October 23rd, when King Charles III met with the Pope. King Charles III and Pope Leo XIV made history by **praying side by side** in the Sistine Chapel — the first time a British monarch and a pope have done so in almost **five centuries**, since the **Reformation** in the 16th century.

Under Michelangelo’s _Last Judgment_, the two leaders stood together as the Pope said, “Let us pray.” For many, this short sentence symbolised the end of a religious divide that began when **Henry VIII** broke away from Rome and created the Church of England in 1534.

The **ecumenical prayer service**, held in Latin and English, brought together **Catholic and Anglican** traditions. Choirs from Windsor Castle, St James’s Palace, and the Sistine Chapel filled the room with music. The Archbishop of York and several senior Catholic bishops joined the ceremony, showing the shared commitment to **Christian unity and care for the environment**.

The theme of the service — the **protection of nature** — reflected both leaders’ priorities. King Charles has long campaigned for action on climate change, while the Vatican continues the mission started by _Pope Francis_ with his encyclical _Laudato Si’_, a call to care for our common home, the Earth.

But the visit wasn’t only about religion. It came at a delicate time for the royal family, as the media continued to question Prince Andrew’s past links to Jeffrey Epstein. The King even joked to the Pope that the cameras were a “constant hazard,” to which Leo XIV calmly replied, “You get used to it.”

Later, the King and Queen visited **St Paul Outside the Walls**, one of the great basilicas of Rome, where they prayed at the tomb of Saint Paul and Charles was named _Royal Confrater_ — a symbolic title meaning _spiritual brother_.

In the end, this day was about **reconciliation** — closing a divide that began 500 years ago. Once rivals, the two churches now stand together, united in prayer, art, and a shared vision for peace and the planet.

Last week, one of the most discussed international stories comes from the United States. President Donald Trump has announced that he wants to restart nuclear testing, something the country has not done for more than thirty years.

The last U.S. nuclear explosion took place in 1992 at the Nevada Test Site. Since then, American scientists have relied on computer modeling and small “subcritical” experiments — tests that stop just before creating an actual explosion. Nuclear testing was banned internationally by the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty in 1996, although the United States never ratified it.

Trump’s announcement immediately created confusion and concern among experts. Many say that physical testing is outdated, unnecessary, and extremely expensive. Restarting the program could take years and cost more than one hundred million dollars. The test site itself would need major renovation — much of the equipment is rusted, and many of the skilled workers who once operated it have retired or left.

The administration argues that new tests are needed to keep up with countries like Russia, China, and North Korea, which have modernized their nuclear arsenals in recent years. Supporters believe the United States must show that it is ready to act if its rivals resume testing. But critics warn that restarting tests could break a thirty-year global taboo and spark a new arms race at a moment when global tensions are already rising.

The decision also raises legal and diplomatic questions. While the treaty banning nuclear tests has not been ratified by the U.S., it remains one of the strongest symbols of international commitment to limit nuclear weapons. Many analysts believe that even one American test could encourage other countries to do the same.

So far, the White House has not provided technical details. It is unclear whether Trump’s order involves full-scale nuclear explosions or smaller experiments. The announcement came just hours before a meeting with Chinese leader Xi Jinping, adding even more uncertainty to an already complex geopolitical situation.

For now, the Nevada desert remains quiet — but the debate over America’s nuclear future is anything but calm.
